How a one-percent distortion weighed the universe.

Mass lying between us and a distant galaxy distorts the galaxy’s image by about one percent. Weak gravitational lensing uses that distortion to weigh matter that cannot be seen. This history follows how an effect first treated as a nuisance became a measurement.

The twelve chapters run from a Bavarian surveyor’s calculation of light bending past the sun to the shear catalogues of Euclid, Rubin and Roman. They can be reached from the rail above or read in sequence. The order follows the development of ideas more closely than the calendar, so Chapter 7 goes back to 1984 to treat galaxy–galaxy lensing in one place. Each chapter can be read on its own, and longer digressions are placed in expandable notes.

Chapter 01 1704 – 1919

The prehistory

Long before anyone discussed shear, the question was simply whether gravity bends light. The two answers, given a century apart, differ by exactly a factor of two.

Newton had already wondered, in the Queries appended to his Opticks of 1704, whether bodies act upon light at a distance and bend its rays. If light is treated as a stream of corpuscles, the answer follows from Newtonian mechanics. John Michell argued in 1783 that light must be subject to gravity; Henry Cavendish worked the deflection out in unpublished notes soon afterwards; and Johann Georg von Soldner did the calculation in 1801 and published it in the Berliner Astronomisches Jahrbuch for 1804, printed that same year. He found that a ray grazing the limb of the sun should be deflected by a little under an arcsecond, 0.87 in modern units.

In 1911 Einstein, working from the equivalence principle alone, obtained the same value as the Newtonian calculation. He therefore had the deflection of starlight as a testable prediction four years before the field equations were finished, and an expedition to the Crimea set out in 1914 to measure it. The outbreak of war stopped the expedition, and the astronomers were briefly interned. Had the observation been made, a correct measurement would have contradicted the theory Einstein then held.

General relativity doubles the deflection, to 1.75 arcseconds at the limb, because the theory curves space as well as time. The Newtonian calculation accounts for only half of the effect.

On 29 May 1919, Eddington’s party on Príncipe and Crommelin and Davidson’s at Sobral, both organised by Frank Dyson from Greenwich, photographed the Hyades stars around an eclipsed sun and found the relativistic value. The result established the deflection of light, was widely reported, and made Einstein a public figure.

Astronomers saw quickly that a deflecting mass is a crude lens. Eddington noted in 1920 that a second image should form; Oliver Lodge had used the words “gravitational lens” in 1919, to say the sun does not act as one in the ordinary sense; Orest Chwolson described the symmetric ring in 1924. None of them yet reversed the argument. If mass bends light by a calculable amount, then the bending of light measures the mass, including mass that gives off no light at all. That reversal is the basis of weak lensing. It was stated thirteen years later by Fritz Zwicky, a Swiss astronomer known for his abrasive manner.

+ More detail: why the factor of two

In the weak-field metric, both the time–time and the space–space potentials contribute to the trajectory of a null geodesic. The Newtonian calculation captures only the first, which is why Soldner and Einstein in 1911 agree with each other but not with observation. Lensing responds to the sum of the two potentials, known as the Weyl potential.

This is also why lensing constrains modified-gravity theories differently from dynamics. A star orbiting in a galaxy responds to the time–time potential alone; a photon crossing it responds to the sum. A theory can be adjusted to reproduce rotation curves and still predict the wrong lensing signal, a point that Chapter 11 takes up.

Chapter 02 1936 – 1937

Zwicky’s proposal

Einstein published the lensing formula in Science and privately said it had little value. Within a year Fritz Zwicky had explained, in a single page, why lensing would become one of the most useful methods in astronomy.

Fig. 2  Zwicky (1937), Phys. Rev. 51, 290, “Nebulae as Gravitational Lenses”, a single page in two columns. Nearly all of its predictions have since been borne out.

Einstein wrote his 1936 note reluctantly. A Czech amateur named Rudi Mandl had been pressing him about stellar lensing for months, and Einstein eventually worked out the ring and the magnification and sent them to Science. He told the editor privately that the paper had little value and that he had written it only to keep Mandl quiet. His reasoning was sound on its own terms: for one star lensing another, the ring would be milliarcseconds across, far too small to resolve, and the alignments would be extremely rare.

Zwicky, writing in Physical Review the following year, changed the lens. “Extragalactic nebulae”, that is, galaxies and clusters of galaxies, are far more massive than stars and far larger on the sky, so both the angular scale of the effect and the probability of a chance alignment grow by orders of magnitude. An effect that could not be observed for stars might be observable for galaxies.

He then listed three uses, which amount to a research programme. First, lensing would provide a new test of general relativity. Second, magnification would let astronomers see objects too distant to observe otherwise. Third, and with the largest consequences, lensing would give the masses of nebulae directly.

The third point underlies everything that follows. Deflection depends only on mass, and is indifferent to whether the mass shines. Zwicky had argued in 1933 from the velocity dispersion of the Coma cluster that it contained far more mass than its galaxies could account for, so he already had reason to want a mass measurement that did not depend on light. In effect, he was proposing a way to weigh dark matter.

What he had in mind, however, was strong lensing, which produces multiple images and rings and is rare. The idea of recovering mass from small statistical distortions of thousands of ordinary background galaxies appeared only about thirty years later. There was also nothing yet to observe: no gravitational lens of any kind was found until 1979.

+ More detail: the question of priority

Einstein was not first, and knew it. His own notebooks, found and published by Renn, Sauer and Stachel in 1997, show he had worked out the lensing formula in 1912, three years before general relativity was complete, and never published it. Eddington described the double image in 1920, Oliver Lodge used the phrase “gravitational lens” in 1919, and Orest Chwolson published the ring in 1924. A Soviet strand of work, by Tikhov and later Klimov, ran in parallel and was largely unread in the west.

Zwicky’s contribution lay elsewhere. He recognised that the formula had been applied to the wrong objects, and that its main use would be to measure mass.

Chapter 03 1937 – 1964

The dormant decades

Zwicky’s 1937 proposal was followed by almost thirty years in which little was written on lensing.

Three problems stood in the way: the observations were beyond the instruments of the time, the distance scale of the universe was badly wrong, and general relativity had few active practitioners.

All three eased in the 1960s, and theorists returned to the subject soon afterwards.

The observations were out of reach

Zwicky’s predicted deflections for galaxies were of order an arcsecond, comparable to the blurring caused by the atmosphere. Astronomers of the 1930s to 1950s worked with photographic plates, which are insensitive, nonlinear and hard to measure precisely. In addition, a strong lens needs a bright, distant source almost exactly behind a massive foreground object, and nobody knew of any population of very distant, compact, bright sources. Without a plausible target there was nothing to search for.

The scale of the universe was wrong

Hubble’s 1929 value of the Hubble constant was about 500 km/s/Mpc, and values above 500 were still in use in the 1940s, several times the modern value. Baade’s recalibration of the Cepheid scale in 1952 halved it, and Sandage’s work later that decade brought it down towards 75. Distances were correspondingly underestimated and the universe seemed younger and smaller than it is. Lensing strength depends on the geometry linking observer, lens and source, so every quantitative prediction rested on a badly wrong distance scale. Known galaxies also sat at modest redshifts, so there was no obvious population of “background” objects far behind the “foreground” ones.

Relativity was out of fashion

The historian Jean Eisenstaedt (1989) calls roughly 1925 to 1955 general relativity’s “low water mark,” and Clifford Will calls what followed its “renaissance.” After the excitement of 1919 the theory came to be regarded as mathematically admirable but of little use to experiment, with a handful of tests and very few practitioners. Physics was occupied with quantum mechanics, nuclear structure and, through the war years, with weapons. A subfield that depended on a neglected theory had little to attract researchers. Even the tests it had were few: the perihelion of Mercury, the eclipse deflection, and a gravitational redshift that was not convincingly measured until 1960.

Quasars were identified in 1963. They are bright, compact and very distant, the kind of background source that lensing calculations needed. That December the first Texas Symposium on Relativistic Astrophysics convened in Dallas to discuss objects that could not be understood without general relativity, and within a few years the cosmic microwave background and pulsars had arrived as well. Within a few years astronomers had several kinds of object that could not be explained without general relativity.

The theorists returned to lensing almost at once. Klimov wrote on galaxies as lenses in 1963 and Liebes on stellar lensing in 1964. The most important of these papers was Sjur Refsdal’s in 1964. He showed that the time delay between two images of a variable source measures both the mass of the lens and the Hubble constant, which made lensing a possible way to measure cosmological parameters.

Two things were still missing, and they set the pace of the next twenty-five years. There were no detectors capable of measuring faint galaxy shapes precisely, which had to wait for CCDs in the late 1970s. Nor was it yet known that the sky is densely covered with faint distant galaxies whose shapes could be averaged; that discovery is the subject of Chapter 5.

+ More detail: Refsdal’s time delays and their later use

Refsdal’s argument runs as follows. Two images of the same lensed source take paths of different length through different depths of the gravitational potential, so a change in the source shows up in one image before the other. The lag depends on the geometry and hence on the Hubble constant. Measure the delay and the lens model, and you have H₀ without a distance ladder.

This was proposed fifteen years before any lens was known. The double quasar of 1979 finally supplied a candidate, but the delay was contested for nearly two decades, until Kundić et al. (1997) settled it at about 417 days, largely because the lens mass model and the delay trade off against one another. Only with well-observed lensed quasars and, more recently, lensed supernovae has the method become competitive with other measurements of H₀, some forty years after Refsdal proposed it.

Chapter 04 1966 – 1967

The theoretical seed

Earlier work had treated lensing as a rare accident of alignment. Two papers then treated it as something that happens to every ray crossing a lumpy universe, and the idea of weak lensing begins with them, twenty years before the name existed.

By 1966 cosmology was becoming an observational science. The microwave background had been found the year before, the steady-state debate was ending, and the field was built on Friedmann–Robertson–Walker models that assume the universe is perfectly smooth. This raised two questions: how could observations show whether that assumption holds, and what does real lumpiness do to what we see?

The mathematics for answering it already existed. Around 1961 Rainer Sachs had derived what are now called the optical scalar equations, which describe how a narrow bundle of light rays evolves as it travels through curved spacetime. They separate the effect of gravity on the bundle into two pieces, and that separation is the conceptual framework of weak lensing.

Matter inside the beam contributes Ricci curvature, which focuses the bundle isotropically. An image is magnified but its shape is unchanged. Matter outside the beam contributes Weyl curvature, the tidal part of gravity, which shears the bundle and stretches a circular image into an ellipse. In the language the field later adopted, these are convergence and shear. The mathematics existed before anyone regarded it as a subject of study.

Two kinds of curvature
Ricci → κ  ·  Weyl → γ

Matter in the beam magnifies without distorting; matter outside it distorts without magnifying. The two effects were named convergence and shear thirty years later.

Kristian & Sachs (1966)

Jerome Kristian was an observer and Sachs a relativist, and “Observations in Cosmology” is an attempt to do cosmology without assuming FRW. They expanded observable quantities (redshifts, distances, number counts, image shapes) as power series around the observer in a general spacetime, then asked which observations would betray departures from homogeneity. One result is that Weyl curvature along the line of sight distorts the images of distant galaxies coherently: neighbouring galaxies are stretched in similar directions. Measuring that pattern would map the tidal field of the intervening universe, which is what cosmic shear surveys do today.

The problem of intrinsic shapes

They also identified the difficulty that would limit the subject for the next twenty-five years. Galaxies are not intrinsically round, so any lensing distortion sits buried in the random scatter of intrinsic shapes. Their estimates made the effect small, and with photographic plates it looked impractical. Kristian (1967) did attempt a search for distortions in images of distant galaxies, and the data of the day could not come close to the required precision. The difficulty is statistical: a percent-level distortion against a thirty-percent scatter needs thousands of galaxies per measurement, and plates could not deliver reliable shapes for even hundreds.

Gunn (1967)

James Gunn, then a young theorist at Caltech fresh from the Gunn–Peterson paper, wrote two papers on light propagation through an inhomogeneous universe. He concentrated instead on how matter near the line of sight randomly magnifies, demagnifies and distorts distant sources. He framed this partly as a limitation, since lensing adds noise to the angular sizes and brightnesses cosmologists were using to measure geometry, but he also recognised that the same effect carries information about the intervening mass, including the dark part. The two papers also worked out the statistics of the fluctuations, treating the intervening matter as a random field. This is the first appearance of the averaging on which cosmic shear rests.

Both views are still in use. Supernova cosmologists treat lensing magnification as a noise term to be modelled, while weak lensing surveys treat the same physics as the signal.

Gunn’s work also connects to a parallel argument about how to average a clumpy universe. Zel’dovich had asked in 1964 what happens to light in a universe where matter is concentrated into galaxies, so that a typical beam travels through mostly empty space. Dyer and Roeder developed this “empty beam” distance in the early 1970s, and Weinberg showed in 1976 that flux is conserved when averaged over all sources, so magnification and demagnification balance. These arguments about averaging anticipate the statistical reasoning on which the field came to depend.

The three ideas that define weak lensing all appear in this work. The large-scale matter distribution distorts every distant image, not only the rare aligned ones. The distortion is coherent, and therefore statistically measurable across many galaxies. And the random intrinsic shapes of galaxies are the dominant obstacle, so very large numbers of galaxies are needed. The first two ideas suggested a measurement; the third explains why it was not made until 1990.

+ More detail: the empty-beam debate

If most of a light beam’s path is through underdense space, with the matter concentrated into lumps it rarely hits, then the typical source is demagnified relative to the smooth-universe prediction, while the rare well-aligned source is strongly magnified. Dyer and Roeder parametrised this with a “smoothness” factor and produced distance–redshift relations that differ appreciably from the FRW ones at high redshift.

Weinberg’s 1976 argument constrains all such schemes: photon number is conserved, so averaged over all sources at a given redshift, the mean magnification must be unity. The distribution is skewed, with many sources slightly demagnified and a few strongly magnified, but it cannot be biased overall. Getting this right matters whenever a measurement depends on the mean rather than the median, which is why it resurfaced in the supernova cosmology of the late 1990s.

Chapter 05 1979 – 1990

The first detection

Strong lensing, in the form of multiple quasar images and cluster arcs, was observed first. The weak signal, a one-percent tangential alignment of faint blue galaxies behind a cluster, was measured in CCD data in 1990.

The first lens of any kind was found in 1979, when Walsh, Carswell and Weymann identified the twin quasar Q0957+561 as one object seen twice. Then, in 1986–87, Lynds & Petrosian and, independently, Soucail and collaborators found long, thin luminous arcs in clusters such as Abell 370. Paczyński and others proposed that these were strongly lensed background galaxies, and a redshift from Soucail’s team in 1988 confirmed that the arc in Abell 370 lay far behind the cluster. Smaller “arclets” turned up soon after.

In hindsight, the arcs imply weak lensing. If a cluster can stretch a few well-placed galaxies into arcs near its core, then at larger radii it must stretch all the background galaxies slightly. Weak lensing is the same distortion further from the core, where it is too small to see in a single galaxy and can only be measured statistically.

Tyson, Valdes and Wenk made the measurement in 1990, in a short Astrophysical Journal Letters paper titled “Detection of Systematic Gravitational Lens Galaxy Image Alignments: Mapping Dark Matter in Galaxy Clusters.” They imaged two clusters, Abell 1689 (massive and nearby, at redshift 0.18) and the more distant CL 1409+52, and measured the orientations of the faint blue galaxies around each.

The signature they sought is tangential alignment. A mass concentration stretches background images perpendicular to the direction pointing toward it, so around a cluster the galaxies should line up like the rings of a target. The orientation of any single galaxy is dominated by its own intrinsic shape and says nothing about the lens. Averaged over many galaxies, a statistically significant tangential pattern appeared in both clusters.

From the strength and radial dependence of that pattern they estimated the mass distributions. They concluded that both clusters hold large amounts of dark matter, with high mass-to-light ratios, and that the dark matter is distributed more smoothly than the galaxies. This was the first time a cluster had been weighed through gravity’s effect on light alone, without assuming that either the galaxy velocities or the hot gas were in equilibrium.

The right-hand panel is the realistic one. The measurement is the average over thousands of galaxies, which is why the idea waited twenty-four years for detectors that could deliver enough of them.

CCDs, and software to match

Tony Tyson, at Bell Labs, was among the first astronomers to use CCDs for deep imaging, and with John Jarvis built FOCAS, one of the earliest systems for automatically detecting and measuring faint objects. Weak lensing needs the shapes of thousands of galaxies measured the same way every time, which cannot be done by eye on photographic plates. FOCAS detected objects, measured their positions, magnitudes and second moments, and classified them as stars or galaxies, all without a human looking at the frame.

A dense population of sources

Deep counts by Tyson and Jarvis (1979), from digitised photographic plates, and then CCD counts by Tyson (1988) revealed a large population of faint blue galaxies, hundreds of thousands per square degree at the faintest magnitudes. Their redshifts were unknown, but their faintness and sheer number suggested most were distant. They therefore provided a dense set of background sources behind any foreground cluster. They were also bluer than nearby galaxies of similar brightness, which is where the name came from.

Two earlier attempts

Tyson’s group had already made two unsuccessful attempts. Valdes, Tyson & Jarvis (1983) looked for coherent alignments across random fields (cosmic shear, in modern terms) and obtained only upper limits. Tyson et al. (1984) tried individual foreground galaxies and found no signal. The two null results forced the group to understand the systematics of shape measurement and suggested that a stronger lens was needed; clusters were the natural candidates.

The analysis was approximate, and its gaps set the agenda for the next five years. There was no general way to turn a measured shear field into a two-dimensional mass map, so they fitted simple models instead; Kaiser & Squires supplied such a method in 1993. Shape measurement was also approximate: the telescope’s point spread function smears images and can itself elongate them, faking or diluting a signal, and a rigorous correction came only with KSB in 1995.

The source redshifts were unknown, and lensing strength depends on how far behind the lens the sources sit, so the mass normalisation was uncertain, especially for the more distant cluster. And shear alone cannot distinguish a mass distribution from the same distribution plus a uniform sheet of mass, the mass-sheet degeneracy that later work had to handle explicitly.

The paper nonetheless established that the Kristian–Sachs idea works: coherent shape distortions of faint galaxies can be detected and used to map invisible mass. Its method (deep imaging, a dense population of background galaxies, careful shape measurement and statistical averaging) is the one the field still follows.

+ More detail: what the “blue fog” was

The faint blue galaxies turned out to be mostly distant. Nobody knew that at the time, and identifying them became one of the central puzzles of 1980s and 1990s extragalactic astronomy, the “faint blue galaxy problem.”

When deep CCD counts arrived there were far too many faint galaxies. “No-evolution” models take the local galaxy population, distribute it through an expanding universe and predict how many galaxies should appear at each magnitude. Compared with these models, the observed counts at faint blue magnitudes exceeded predictions by factors of several, and the excess objects were bluer than typical nearby galaxies. Blue light indicates young massive stars, so these galaxies were forming stars rapidly.

The natural test was to measure redshifts, and the first results were puzzling. Spectroscopic surveys reaching blue magnitudes of 22 to 24, by Broadhurst, Ellis & Shanks (1988) and Colless et al. (1990), found the faint blue galaxies were not especially distant, with few beyond redshift 0.5, even though there were too many of them. That suggested an excess of low-luminosity star-forming galaxies at modest redshifts: perhaps dwarfs in a burst that have since faded or merged.

This mattered a great deal to Tyson. If much of the fog sat at low redshift, many “background” galaxies were not far behind the cluster at all, which weakens the signal and corrupts the mass. His interpretation depended on the fainter galaxies, beyond the reach of those spectrographs, being substantially more distant.

The question was settled during the 1990s by several lines of evidence. Deeper spectroscopy with larger telescopes, including the Canada–France Redshift Survey and Keck surveys led by Cowie, showed the redshift distribution climbing as you go fainter, with median redshifts approaching 1 at the depths lensing uses. The Hubble Deep Field in 1995 showed many faint blue galaxies to be small, irregular and disturbed, unlike orderly nearby spirals and ellipticals. Steidel’s Lyman-break technique found large numbers of star-forming galaxies at redshift 3. And Lilly et al. (1996) and Madau et al. (1996) showed the cosmic star formation rate had been many times higher in the past, peaking around redshifts 1 to 3.

So the fog is a mixture, dominated by star-forming galaxies across a broad range of redshifts, with the typical object in a deep image sitting near redshift 1. It looks blue because the universe was forming stars far faster then, and because redshift moves their strong ultraviolet emission into blue filters. The episode showed that the signal depends on the redshift distribution of the sources, and that getting that distribution wrong biases the mass. Early cluster studies had to guess it; modern surveys spend a great deal of effort calibrating it, and it remains one of the dominant systematic uncertainties.

+ More detail: why the counts exceeded the no-evolution models

Galaxies in the past were brighter in blue light, probably more numerous as small separate objects, and the models rested on a local census and a cosmology that both undercounted what should be visible. It took about a decade to establish how much each factor contributed.

Luminosity evolution was the main factor. Star formation was far more vigorous around redshift 1 than today, and young massive stars are luminous and blue, so a vigorously star-forming galaxy outshines its present-day self, especially in the blue. Brighter galaxies are visible to greater distances, which fills the faint counts with objects the models say should be too dim. The Canada–France Redshift Survey showed this directly: the luminosity function of blue galaxies brightened substantially by redshift 0.5 to 1, while red galaxies barely changed.

The k-correction added to the effect. A blue filter observing a galaxy at redshift 1 sees its rest-frame ultraviolet. Models assuming local-type spectra, which are faint in the ultraviolet, badly underestimate how long star-forming galaxies stay visible in blue images.

An important clue came from the infrared. Counts in the near-infrared K band, which traces the older stars carrying most of a galaxy’s mass, sat fairly close to the no-evolution predictions. The excess was therefore due mainly to additional star formation, which shows up in blue light, and not to additional mass. This favoured luminosity evolution over more exotic explanations.

Smaller galaxies may have been more numerous. In hierarchical structure formation, today’s large galaxies were assembled from smaller ones, so there were more separate galaxies in the past. Babul and Rees (1992) went further and proposed a population of dwarfs that burst into star formation around redshift 1 and then faded strongly. The proposal was an attempt to explain why the Broadhurst–Ellis–Shanks redshift distribution looked so unremarkable despite the excess. HST later confirmed that many faint blue galaxies are small and irregular, though the extreme fading scenario was never clearly established.

The local census was incomplete and the cosmology was wrong. Low surface brightness dwarfs are easy to miss nearby, so the faint end of the local luminosity function was probably steeper than assumed. And most models assumed a flat matter-only universe, which contains relatively little volume at high redshift. A universe with a cosmological constant holds substantially more volume at redshifts 1 to 3, and therefore more galaxies. Fukugita and collaborators argued in 1990 that faint counts favoured a cosmological constant, eight years before the supernova results made the idea widely accepted. In hindsight it accounts for part of the excess, though not all of it.

Chapter 06 1992 – 1995

The theory becomes a tool

Kaiser and Squires showed that a shear field can be inverted into a map of surface density without a model of the lens or any assumption about its form. With that method, and a way of measuring galaxy shapes that followed in 1995, weak lensing became a practical means of measuring mass.

Nick Kaiser was already known for two ideas that share a preoccupation: galaxy biasing (1984), which explains why clusters cluster more strongly than the matter does, and redshift-space distortions (1987), which use peculiar velocities to measure the matter density. Both address the same problem: galaxies are imperfect tracers of mass, which leaves open how the mass itself can be measured. Lensing was a natural next step.

In the early 1990s the central question in cosmology was the matter density: theorists favoured Ω = 1 on grounds of inflation and simplicity, while many observations pointed to 0.2 or 0.3. Cluster mass-to-light ratios were a key input, since the mean M/L times the universe’s total luminosity estimates Ω. But cluster masses from galaxy velocities assume dynamical equilibrium, and masses from ROSAT X-ray data assume the hot gas is in hydrostatic equilibrium. Lensing assumes neither, so a method for mapping mass with it bore directly on the question. COBE’s 1992 detection of microwave background fluctuations added urgency by making structure formation models testable.

Kaiser (1992): shear as a power spectrum

“Weak Gravitational Lensing of Distant Galaxies” worked out the statistics of distortions produced by large-scale structure rather than by a single cluster. Its technical core is a Fourier-space version of the Limber approximation, relating the angular power spectrum of the shear to the three-dimensional matter power spectrum weighted along the line of sight. Blandford, Saust, Brainerd & Villumsen (1991) and Miralda-Escudé (1991) reached similar conclusions at nearly the same moment, but Kaiser’s formalism became the standard language, and Chapter 8 returns to it. The paper treated the sources as lying on a single redshift shell, an approximation later replaced by the full source distribution.

Kaiser & Squires (1993): the inversion

“Mapping the Dark Matter with Weak Gravitational Lensing” had the most lasting influence of these papers. Both the convergence κ and the shear γ are second derivatives of one lensing potential, so in Fourier space the derivatives become multiplications and κ can be written directly in terms of the measured shear. Equivalently, in real space the mass map is the shear field convolved with a kernel falling as 1/θ². Tyson’s group had fitted models; Kaiser and Squires made it possible to see how the dark matter was distributed without assuming a shape for it.

KSB (1995): measuring the shapes

The inversion requires accurate galaxy shapes, and this paper provided a way to measure them. Kaiser, Squires & Broadhurst built a PSF correction from weighted second moments of each galaxy’s light profile, treating the PSF as a small perturbation and calculating how each galaxy’s ellipticity responds to it (the smear polarisability) and to a shear (the shear polarisability). Stars in the same image are point sources, so their measured shapes reveal the PSF anisotropy directly, which can be interpolated to each galaxy and subtracted. Luppino & Kaiser (1997) completed it with a correction for the isotropic blurring that dilutes the signal.

Kaiser and Squires also dealt with the practical consequences. Intrinsic ellipticities act as noise, so they derived the noise in the reconstructed map, set by the number of background galaxies per unit area; the formula told observers how deep they needed to observe. They also noted a consistency check that comes at no extra cost: because the true shear field derives from a single potential, a “rotated” component of the signal should vanish, and a nonzero one indicates systematic error. That diagnostic, the B-mode, became central to every survey that followed.

The original inversion had known weaknesses, and much of 1994 to 1996 went into fixing them. The Fourier inversion assumes shear is measured everywhere, but real images have edges; Fahlman, Kaiser, Squires & Woods (1994) introduced an aperture densitometry statistic measuring the mean mass inside a circle relative to a surrounding annulus, using only shear in the annulus. Applied to MS 1224+20, it gave a mass-to-light ratio of around 800 in solar units, well above other estimates. Schneider (1996) generalised the idea into the aperture mass.

What galaxies actually record is not γ but the reduced shear g = γ/(1 − κ), which differs noticeably near cluster cores; Kaiser (1995) and Seitz & Schneider (1995) developed nonlinear reconstructions to handle it. And adding a uniform sheet of mass, with a rescaling, leaves the reduced shear unchanged, a degeneracy that Falco, Gorenstein & Shapiro had identified for strong lenses in 1985. Broadhurst, Taylor & Peacock (1995) proposed breaking it with magnification, which changes the number counts of background galaxies behind a cluster.

“KSB” was the standard method for more than a decade, used in most early cluster studies and in several of the 2000 cosmic shear detections. Its approximations eventually became its limitation, since a perturbative treatment is not exact for realistic PSFs and galaxy profiles. The shape-measurement challenges of Chapter 10 grew largely out of efforts to understand and replace it.

+ More detail: the Kaiser–Squires inversion in seven steps

1. Everything comes from one potential. A light ray passing a mass distribution is deflected by an angle that is the gradient of a two-dimensional lensing potential ψ, which is the projected Newtonian potential scaled by the distances. It obeys a 2D Poisson equation, ∇²ψ = 2κ, where κ is the projected surface density divided by a critical density set by the lens and source distances.

2. How images are distorted. A small image is mapped from source plane to image plane by the Jacobian of the lens mapping, built from the second derivatives of ψ: κ = ½(∂₁² + ∂₂²)ψ, γ₁ = ½(∂₁² − ∂₂²)ψ, γ₂ = ∂₁∂₂ψ. The trace magnifies uniformly; the traceless part stretches into an ellipse. Shear has two components because an ellipse’s orientation matters. The important point is that ψ has only three second derivatives, and κ, γ₁ and γ₂ are three combinations of them, so all three are determined by the single function ψ.

3. In Fourier space the problem becomes simple. Each derivative becomes a multiplication, the equations become algebraic, and ψ can be eliminated entirely. Writing the shear as a complex number and the wavevector by its length and angle φ, the result is γ̂(ℓ) = e2iφ κ̂(ℓ). For every Fourier mode, shear equals convergence times a pure phase, of magnitude one and rotating by twice the wavevector’s angle. The factor of two is there because shear is a spin-2 quantity: an ellipse looks the same after a 180° rotation. The inversion follows directly: κ̂(ℓ) = e−2iφ γ̂(ℓ). Measure the shear, transform, rotate each mode’s phase, transform back, and the result is a map of projected mass, obtained without a model and without assumptions about equilibrium, symmetry, or how mass relates to light.

4. The real-space picture. The same operation is a convolution with a kernel falling as 1/|θ|² and carrying a spin-2 angular pattern. In practice: to estimate the mass at a point, add up the tangential shear of the galaxies around it, weighting the nearer ones more heavily. The tangential alignment that Tyson’s group measured is therefore built into the inversion.

5. From galaxies to a map. Each measured ellipticity is the lensing shear plus the galaxy’s intrinsic shape. If intrinsic orientations are random they average to zero, so the mean ellipticity in a small patch estimates the local shear; Kaiser and Squires replaced the integral with a sum over galaxies, smoothed to keep the noise finite.

6. The noise works out simply. Because the Fourier kernel has magnitude one, intrinsic shapes produce white noise in the shear and the inversion passes it straight through, so the mass map’s noise is white too. A map smoothed on a scale θs has σκ² ∼ σε² / (n θs²), where σε ≈ 0.3 per component is the intrinsic scatter and n the number of background galaxies per unit area. The relation told observers what they needed: more galaxies per square arcminute give finer or less noisy maps. It is a large part of the reason weak lensing pushed observers toward deeper imaging and better seeing.

7. A consistency check, and a limitation of the inversion. The shear field has two components but derives from one scalar function, so the system is overdetermined, and the redundancy is a test: gravity produces only E-modes, and the B-mode should vanish up to noise. Rotating every galaxy by 45° swaps the two and switches the real signal off. The phase factor is also undefined at ℓ = 0, because a perfectly uniform sheet of mass produces no shear at all, so the inversion recovers κ only up to an additive constant. That is the mass-sheet degeneracy in its simplest form.

+ More detail: the mass-sheet degeneracy

Adding a uniform sheet of convergence and rescaling the source plane leaves the reduced shear unchanged, so shear alone cannot fix the absolute normalisation of a mass map. Magnification, number counts, or an assumption that the field is empty at large radius will break it, and every cluster mass derived from lensing depends on which of these is used.

Chapter 07 1984 – 2006

Galaxy–galaxy lensing

Cluster lensing weighs the largest objects in the universe. Galaxy–galaxy lensing asks the same question of ordinary ones: how much dark matter surrounds a typical galaxy, and how far out does it go?

By the late 1970s flat rotation curves had convinced most astronomers that spiral galaxies sit inside dark halos. But rotation curves run out of tracers. Beyond the edge of a galaxy’s neutral hydrogen, typically a few tens of kiloparsecs, there is nothing left to measure, so the halo’s extent and total mass were unknown. Since a flat rotation curve means mass grows in proportion to radius, whether a halo stops at 30 kpc or 300 kpc is a tenfold difference in its mass. Satellite galaxy motions and binary galaxy dynamics pointed toward large halos, but on small samples and with assumptions about orbits.

Lensing can probe those radii directly. The difficulty is that a single galaxy is a very weak lens: its shear on background galaxies tens of arcseconds away is well under one percent, far below the scatter in intrinsic shapes. It cannot be detected around any one galaxy, so the measurement has to be made by stacking, that is, by averaging the tangential alignment of background galaxies around many thousands of foreground ones.

The first attempt was made by Tyson, Valdes, Jarvis & Mills in 1984. Using digitised photographic plates from the 4-metre Mayall telescope covering tens of thousands of galaxies, they split the sample by brightness, treated the brighter galaxies as foreground lenses and the fainter as background sources, and looked for tangential alignment.

They found no signal. From the upper limit they argued that galaxy halos must be smaller or less massive than extended-halo models suggested, which would have been an important result. Kovner and Milgrom reanalysed the problem in 1987 and argued the limits were considerably weaker than claimed, partly because of assumptions about source redshifts and the overlap in distance between lenses and sources. The eventual consensus was that the experiment lacked the sensitivity: photographic plates, modest seeing and unknown redshifts could not reach the required precision. As with the cosmic shear limits, this null result pushed Tyson’s group toward the stronger signal from clusters.

The first convincing detection came in 1996, from Tereasa Brainerd, Roger Blandford and Ian Smail. Their data were deep CCD imaging from the Palomar 5-metre, covering a single field but with far better image quality than the 1984 plates. Like Tyson they had no redshifts, so they used magnitude as a proxy, treating a brighter range as lenses and a fainter range as sources, then measured the mean tangential ellipticity of sources as a function of angular distance from the lenses, over scales of several to a few tens of arcseconds.

They found a significant tangential alignment and checked that it vanished when the sources were rotated by 45°, the E/B test of Kaiser and Squires. Modelling the lenses as isothermal halos with a cutoff radius, and using simulations to handle the unknown redshift distributions, they found halos with circular velocities around 200 km/s, similar to the Milky Way, extending to at least roughly 100 kpc, well beyond the reach of rotation curves. The measurement came from one field and was of modest significance, but it showed that the method worked and that halos are large.

Refinement, 1996–2004

Several groups followed quickly. Dell’Antonio & Tyson (1996) and Hudson and collaborators (1998) applied the method to the Hubble Deep Field, where HST’s sharp images help with small faint galaxies, and Hudson’s group used photometric redshifts to separate lenses from sources properly. Schneider & Rix (1997) developed a maximum-likelihood method using each lens–source pair optimally rather than binning. Griffiths and collaborators (1996) found a similar signal in HST’s Medium Deep Survey, and Natarajan, Kneib, Smail & Ellis (1998) applied the method inside clusters, measuring the halos of cluster galaxies superimposed on the cluster’s own field.

Wide surveys: SDSS and the RCS

Fischer and collaborators (2000) used commissioning data from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey, covering far more area than any earlier study, and detected the signal at high significance out to hundreds of kiloparsecs, concluding that halos extend beyond about 260 h−1 kpc, roughly 370 kpc for h = 0.7. McKay et al. (2001) added SDSS spectroscopic redshifts for the lenses, which let the signal be expressed in physical units and split by luminosity and galaxy type. Hoekstra and collaborators (2004) used the Red-sequence Cluster Survey to fit truncated halos, finding evidence that halos do have finite extents.

The halo-model interpretation

As data improved the interpretation shifted. Early studies modelled each lens as an isolated galaxy with its own halo, but on large scales the stacked signal also includes mass from neighbouring galaxies and from the group or cluster the galaxy lives in. Guzik & Seljak (2001, 2002) and others reframed the measurement through the halo model, separating a “one-halo” term from the galaxy’s own halo and a “two-halo” term from surrounding structure. The two-halo term dominates beyond a megaparsec or so, which is why the outermost points of a stacked profile say more about where galaxies live than about their own halos.

In that language, galaxy–galaxy lensing measures the cross-correlation between galaxies and mass, usually written as the excess surface density ΔΣ. It therefore measures the galaxy–halo connection as well as halo sizes: how luminosity, stellar mass and colour relate to the mass of the halo hosting them. Sheldon et al. (2004) and Mandelbaum et al. (2006a), on large SDSS samples, measured halo mass as a function of these properties and found, among much else, that red galaxies sit in more massive halos than blue galaxies of the same stellar mass.

Its later role in cosmology may be the more important one. Galaxy clustering alone cannot separate the matter density from the way galaxies trace matter, which is Kaiser’s bias problem again. Combining clustering with galaxy–galaxy lensing and cosmic shear breaks that degeneracy. This “3×2-point” combination is the central analysis of every modern survey and the main subject of Chapter 12.

+ More detail: halo shapes, and aligning galaxies with their halos

Simple models, including the isothermal spheres Brainerd, Blandford & Smail used, treat halos as round for convenience. Cold dark matter simulations from the late 1980s onward consistently produced triaxial halos instead, with three different axis lengths, a consequence of how they form: matter falls in along filaments and mergers arrive from particular directions. Halo shape therefore became a test of the dark matter model. Standard CDM predicts noticeably non-spherical halos; baryons cooling into the centre tend to round the inner halo; self-interacting dark matter would round halos further, because collisions isotropise orbits; and MOND, having no halo at all, predicts that the signal far from a galaxy becomes nearly circular.

The method for measuring it, proposed around 2000 by Brainerd & Wright and by Natarajan & Refregier, is to use the visible galaxy as a guide to the orientation of its halo. An individual halo’s orientation cannot be seen, but if halos tend to align with the light of the galaxy inside them, every lens can be rotated so its major axis points the same way and then stack. A flattened halo has more mass along its long axis, so sources near that axis are sheared more strongly than those near the short axis.

Hoekstra, Yee & Gladders (2004) did this with about 45 square degrees of the Red-sequence Cluster Survey, modelling each halo’s ellipticity as a fraction f of the lens galaxy’s observed ellipticity. Their best fit was f ≈ 0.77, meaning halos somewhat rounder than the light but flattened and aligned with it, and they excluded an isotropic signal at 99.5% confidence, implying a typical projected axis ratio near two thirds. They presented it as evidence against MOND-like isotropic signals and in line with CDM.

The result has proved hard to confirm, which is why it is treated here as a hint. Mandelbaum et al. (2006b), with a much larger SDSS sample, found no significant anisotropy overall, with only a weak hint for bright red galaxies, and later work from CFHTLS, CFHTLenS and KiDS has generally found weak or null signals, especially for spirals. There are three reasons for this. First, the visible galaxy is an unreliable guide: simulations show a galaxy’s light is often misaligned with its halo, particularly for disks whose spin may tilt relative to the halo’s long axis, and misalignment washes out the stacked signal even if individual halos are strongly flattened. Second, the signal is very small, a modulation of a shear that is itself small. Third, systematics can mimic it: residual PSF anisotropy, intrinsic alignments of sources near the lens, and contamination from neighbours can all create or cancel an azimuthal pattern. The present picture is that halos around massive ellipticals do appear flattened and roughly aligned with their light, while the situation for spirals remains unsettled.

Chapter 08 1991 – 1999

Cosmic shear predictions

Cosmic shear is weak lensing in its simplest form. It needs no cluster and no stacking, only a random patch of sky and the coherent distortion produced by all the mass along the line of sight. Through the 1990s theorists worked out how large it should be, and their estimates became the targets that observers later met.

Two developments made the work timely. Tyson’s cluster detection and the arrival of wide-field CCD mosaics suggested that percent-level distortions might at last be measurable over useful areas, and cosmology needed a direct measure of the matter power spectrum.

The problem was a degeneracy. COBE had fixed the amplitude of fluctuations on very large scales, but the amplitude on cluster scales, σ₈, depended on the model. Cluster abundance constrained only a combination, roughly σ₈ Ωm0.5, so a low-density universe with strong clustering looked much like a dense universe with weak clustering. Galaxy surveys measured the clustering of light, not mass, and so were entangled with bias. Because lensing responds to mass directly, it offered a way to settle the question of Ω.

Earlier observational attempts had not succeeded. Valdes, Tyson & Jarvis (1983) and Mould, Blandford, Villumsen and collaborators (1994) produced only upper limits; Villumsen (1995) reanalysed the same data, and the field still had no accepted detection. Part of the theorists’ job was to say how deep and how wide a survey had to be in order to succeed.

The 1991 predictions

Three papers appeared almost together: Blandford, Saust, Brainerd & Villumsen, and Miralda-Escudé, both calculating how large-scale structure distorts distant galaxy images, and Kaiser with a Fourier-space treatment. They concluded that the distortion is small but detectable in principle: in plausible cold dark matter models, galaxy ellipticities should be correlated at roughly the percent level on arcminute scales, falling off at larger separations. Miralda-Escudé framed the key statistic as the ellipticity correlation function and showed it to be a weighted projection of the matter power spectrum. All three assumed the sources sit at a single redshift and used linear theory, which is why their amplitudes turned out to be conservative.

The effect of nonlinear growth

The early calculations used the linear power spectrum, but the arcminute scales where the signal is easiest to measure correspond to structures of a few hundred kiloparsecs to a few megaparsecs, where collapse is strongly nonlinear. Jain & Seljak (1997), building on Villumsen (1996) and using the new fitting formulas for the nonlinear power spectrum, found that nonlinear growth increases the signal on those scales by a large factor, giving an RMS shear of one to two percent instead of a fraction of a percent. This made the observers’ task easier, but it also meant that accurate predictions would require modelling nonlinear clustering and, eventually, baryons.

Breaking the degeneracy

Bernardeau, van Waerbeke & Mellier (1997) offered a route around the σ₈–Ωm degeneracy. Nonlinear gravity makes the convergence field non-Gaussian, with rare high peaks from clusters and broad shallow voids. The skewness of κ, suitably normalised, is nearly independent of σ₈ but depends on Ωm, roughly as Ωm−0.8. The variance measures the combination, and the skewness then constrains Ωm alone. This paper began the programme of non-Gaussian statistics that now includes peak counts, Minkowski functionals and machine-learning summaries. The aperture-mass statistic of the following year made the skewness measurable directly from shear, without an intermediate mass map.

The convergence power spectrum
Pκ(ℓ) = (9/4) Ωm² (H₀/c)⁴ ∫ dχ [W(χ)/a(χ)]² Pδ(ℓ/fK(χ); χ)

Kaiser’s version, via the Limber approximation. The Ωm² prefactor and the σ₈² hiding inside Pδ explain why lensing measures a combination of the two parameters and not each separately.

Two features of that formula explain much of what followed. The prefactor and the amplitude of Pδ mean the signal depends mainly on a combination like σ₈ Ωmα. This is the same degeneracy that affects cluster counts, with a different weighting, and it is the reason modern surveys quote S₈. The kernel W peaks roughly halfway to the sources, which is why source redshifts matter so much: the signal rises steeply as the sources are placed further away.

Other papers translated the predictions into survey designs. Schneider, van Waerbeke, Jain & Kruse (1998) proposed measuring the variance of the aperture mass across many randomly placed apertures; the aperture mass acts as a narrow filter in ℓ and separates naturally into E- and B-modes. Kaiser (1998) worked through the noise budget: with σε ≈ 0.3, detecting a one-percent shear takes of order a thousand galaxies per measurement, which pushes toward deep, wide imaging. He argued a few square degrees could detect the signal, and stressed that PSF anisotropy had to be controlled well below it. Stebbins (1996) and Kamionkowski, Babul, Cress & Refregier (1998) extended the formalism to the curved sky.

The last element was tomography. Hu & Tegmark (1999) applied Fisher matrix forecasting, already standard in CMB work, to lensing surveys, showing that lensing plus the CMB could tightly constrain Ωm, the cosmological constant and neutrino masses. Then Hu’s “Power Spectrum Tomography with Weak Lensing” introduced the idea of dividing sources into redshift bins using photometric redshifts and measuring the signal within and across bins. Because the kernel shifts with source distance, the bins weigh structure at different epochs differently, recovering information that projection would otherwise discard. This is how lensing measures the growth of structure over time, and through it dark energy. Every major survey today uses tomography.

These forecasts arrived just as the 1998 supernova results established an accelerating universe, and weak lensing soon became one of the principal proposed probes of dark energy. By 1999 the theorists had supplied the expected amplitude, suitable statistics, a systematics test, a strategy for breaking degeneracies and explicit survey requirements. A detection now depended on wide-field cameras and careful PSF correction.

Because the signal is a projection, all the matter between the observer and the sources contributes to it, weighted by the kernel. Cosmic shear is therefore sensitive to the total matter distribution without an intervening bias parameter, but finding out when the structure grew requires tomography.

Chapter 09 2000 – 2003

The 2000 cosmic shear detections

Between late February and March 2000, four independent groups posted papers within a few weeks of each other, each reporting that galaxy ellipticities in random patches of sky are correlated at the level the theorists had predicted. Thirty-three years after Kristian’s first search, and seventeen after the first upper limit, cosmic shear had been detected.

The near-simultaneous results had common causes, three of which came together in the late 1990s. Wide-field CCD mosaics (the UH8K and then the CFH12K at the Canada–France–Hawaii Telescope, and the Big Throughput Camera on the CTIO 4-metre) could at last image a significant fraction of a square degree per pointing to the necessary depth, where earlier CCDs covered only a few arcminutes. KSB had been tested for five years on clusters, where the signal is ten times stronger, so groups understood its behaviour well enough to push it to the percent level. And the predictions of Chapter 8, especially after Jain and Seljak’s nonlinear boost, said the signal was about one percent on arcminute scales and that a square degree or so of deep imaging should suffice. Any group with suitable data could see that a detection was within reach, and the result was a race.

Bacon, Refregier & Ellis

This team used the William Herschel Telescope on La Palma to observe fourteen separate fields. The fields were small but well separated, which kept them statistically independent. They measured the variance of the average shear in cells a few arcminutes across, correcting shapes with a version of KSB calibrated on simulated images, and found a signal clearly above the level expected from noise and systematics. Their fields were also independent of the CFHT data two of the other groups used, which mattered when the four results were compared.

Kaiser, Wilson & Luppino

This group used UH8K data from CFHT, originally taken for other purposes and covering several fields. In a sense Kaiser was completing his own programme of 1992 and 1998. The paper reported shear correlations and examined the systematics carefully, though it circulated mainly as a preprint and never became the most cited of the four. It argued that PSF anisotropy, not shot noise, would be the limiting factor for the next generation, a forecast that the next chapter bears out.

Van Waerbeke, Mellier and colleagues

The Paris group used CFHT data from both the UH8K and the new CFH12K, covering around two square degrees and of order a hundred thousand galaxies. They measured the shear variance on scales from about one to several arcminutes and showed its decline with scale matched the predicted shape. This group went on to lead VIRMOS-DESCART and later CFHTLS, the first large dedicated surveys.

Wittman, Tyson and collaborators

This team used the Big Throughput Camera on the Blanco telescope to observe three fields of about half a square degree each, and published in Nature. For Tyson the result completed work begun with the 1983 upper limits. They measured the ellipticity correlation out to around half a degree and reported that their data disfavoured the then-standard cold dark matter model with Ωm = 1 and a high normalisation, while sitting comfortably with low-density models.

The main concern was that a one-percent signal could be produced entirely by imperfect PSF correction. The groups deployed several checks, and those checks became standard practice. Rotating all galaxies by 45° converts the lensing E-mode into a B-mode, so the rotated signal should vanish. If residual PSF anisotropy leaks into galaxy shapes, the corrected ellipticities will correlate with the star ellipticities, and the groups showed this cross-correlation was small compared with the signal. They demonstrated that raw PSF anisotropy had a different amplitude and scale dependence from the final result. And a real cosmological signal should look similar in unrelated directions, where telescope effects need not.

The strongest argument was the agreement between the results. Four teams, on different telescopes and with different cameras, observing strategies and analysis codes, found signals of the same amplitude and consistent with the same theory. Instrumental systematics were unlikely to produce that agreement.

The measurements were far too noisy for precision, but the amplitude fit low-density models with σ₈ somewhere around 0.8 to 1, consistent with cluster abundance. The results also arrived as other evidence was converging. The supernova results of 1998–99 had pointed to a cosmological constant, and in April 2000 BOOMERanG, followed soon by MAXIMA, showed from the microwave background that the universe is spatially flat. Cosmic shear added an independent, mass-based measurement that fit the emerging concordance model of Ωm ≈ 0.3 with a cosmological constant, and this is what made the case for dedicating serious telescope time to lensing surveys.

Later work both confirmed and complicated the picture. Confirmations came quickly from the VLT (Maoli et al. 2001) and from HST (Rhodes, Refregier & Groth 2001), whose stable space-based PSF provided a check free of atmospheric effects. Surveys grew to tens of square degrees: Hoekstra, Yee & Gladders (2002) with the Red-sequence Cluster Survey, Jarvis and collaborators (2003) with about 75 square degrees from CTIO.

The B-mode test then began to reveal problems. Van Waerbeke and collaborators, analysing the larger VIRMOS-DESCART dataset, found a nonzero B-mode at some scales. Pen, van Waerbeke & Mellier (2002) developed cleaner E/B separation, and the residual was traced largely to imperfect PSF correction, which showed that KSB was not accurate enough for the next level of precision.

The measured values also scattered. Compilations of early results showed σ₈ ranging from roughly 0.7 to 1.0 at Ωm = 0.3, a spread larger than the quoted errors. Some early lensing values sat above X-ray cluster estimates, which had drifted toward 0.7. In the modern discrepancy discussed in Chapter 12, the difference runs the other way. Much of the early spread came from shape-measurement biases, uncertain source redshift distributions and approximate nonlinear power spectra, the problems on which the next decade was spent.

Chapter 10 2001 – 2012

Systematics come of age

Once the signal had been detected, the question became how precisely it could be measured. The answer depended far less on statistics than on systematics, the effects that mimic or distort the signal, and most of the important ones were identified in this decade.

The accelerating universe raised the stakes. Tomography meant lensing could track the growth of structure over cosmic time, which depends on dark energy in ways distance measurements alone do not capture. The Dark Energy Task Force report (Albrecht et al. 2006) ranked weak lensing among the most powerful probes available, and ambitious surveys were proposed: the Dark Energy Survey, LSST, and the space missions that became Euclid and Roman.

The difficulty was precision. The 2000 detections tolerated errors of ten percent or so. Forecasts for the new surveys showed that to avoid being systematics-limited, shear measurements would need to be accurate to about 0.1%, and photometric redshift distributions would need their means known to a few parts in a thousand. That is an improvement by a factor of a hundred, and it determined much of the decade’s work.

The assumption of random intrinsic shapes

Every weak lensing measurement assumes intrinsic galaxy shapes average to zero, so that any coherent alignment is lensing. But galaxies form in the same tidal fields that do the lensing, and there are good physical reasons to expect alignment: ellipticals may be stretched along the local tidal field, spirals may acquire spins aligned with it through tidal torques. Heavens, Refregier & Heymans (2000) and Croft & Metzler (2000) raised the problem using simulations; Catelan, Kamionkowski & Blandford (2001) introduced the linear alignment model; Crittenden, Natarajan, Pen & Theuns (2001) treated spirals via tidal torque theory.

The II term

Those papers concerned what is now called the II term, the correlation between the intrinsic shapes of galaxies physically close to one another. Since II affects only pairs at similar distances, King & Schneider (2002) and Heymans & Heavens (2003) showed that photometric redshifts allow such pairs to be down-weighted or removed. Estimates of the contamination varied widely, from negligible to a sizeable fraction of the signal, but the problem seemed tractable. The cost is statistical: dropping close pairs removes exactly the small-separation, same-redshift pairs that carry part of the signal, and it needs photometric redshifts good enough to tell close from distant, which in 2002 was itself an open question.

The GI term

Hirata & Seljak (2004) identified the subtler effect. A massive structure tidally aligns the galaxies near it and lenses galaxies far behind it, and the two are correlated because they share a cause. This GI term correlates the gravitational shear of a background galaxy with the intrinsic shape of a foreground one. Its sign matters: in the linear alignment model the foreground galaxy is stretched radially toward the mass while the lensed background galaxy is stretched tangentially around it, so GI is negative and partly cancels the signal. Because it comes from pairs at very different distances, removing close pairs does not reduce it.

Hirata and Seljak estimated the bias could reach tens of percent for plausible alignment amplitudes. A survey that ignored it would underestimate σ₈, which is relevant to the results described in Chapter 12. Mandelbaum et al. (2006) and Hirata et al. (2007) then measured the correlation between galaxy positions and shapes in SDSS and found a clear alignment signal for luminous red galaxies, with no detection for blue ones. Bridle & King (2007) modified the linear model to use the nonlinear matter power spectrum; this nonlinear alignment model is crude but became the default in nearly every survey analysis since. Joachimi et al. (2011) measured how the amplitude depends on luminosity and redshift.

The field settled on two strategies: include an alignment model with free parameters and marginalise over them, which costs statistical power but protects against bias; or exploit the different redshift dependence of lensing and alignments to separate them, the “nulling” approach of Joachimi & Schneider (2008). Intrinsic alignments remain one of the leading theoretical systematics.

Much of the rest of the decade’s work went into shape measurement. The residual B-modes of the early 2000s had shown the limits of KSB, which is a perturbative approximation; realistic PSFs, non-Gaussian galaxy profiles and noisy faint galaxies all break it. Alternatives appeared, including shapelets, which decompose galaxy images into basis functions, and direct model-fitting methods. Nobody knew how accurate any of them was, however, because the true shear in real data is unknown.

The Shear TEsting Programme addressed this with blind tests: simulated images with a known applied shear, distributed to groups who analysed them without knowing the answer. STEP1 (Heymans et al. 2006) compared about a dozen methods on simple ground-based simulations and introduced the parametrisation used ever since. The multiplicative biases spread over more than an order of magnitude, including among KSB implementations, and different implementations of the same method disagreed. The paper’s own summary was that the pipelines then used for cosmic shear were accurate to better than 7%, and that the most successful methods reached percent-level accuracy, which showed that better results were possible. STEP2 (Massey et al. 2007) used more realistic galaxies built from real HST images and a wider range of PSFs; every STEP1 participant now did better than about 5% and the best methods again reached percent level, but the biases depended on galaxy size, magnitude and PSF shape, so averaging to zero was not guaranteed.

The vocabulary STEP gave the field
γmeasured = (1 + m) γtrue + c

Multiplicative bias m and additive bias c. Survey requirements have been written in these two numbers ever since.

GREAT08 (Bridle et al. 2009) took a different approach: an open competition, published partly in the Annals of Applied Statistics, designed to attract statisticians and computer scientists. It reduced the problem to its essentials (a known PSF, simple galaxies and large noise) and asked entrants to measure the shear. Several new approaches performed well, and the challenge clarified what the fundamental difficulties were. GREAT10 and GREAT3 followed with increasingly realistic tests.

Two of those difficulties proved to be fundamental, in the sense that they arise from the measurement itself and not from particular codes. The first is noise bias. Ellipticity is a nonlinear function of the pixel values, so even an unbiased image yields a biased shape estimate once noise is present, and the bias is worst for the faint galaxies that carry much of the signal. The second is model bias: methods that fit a galaxy model get biased shapes when real galaxies refuse to match the model, as galaxies with bulges, spiral arms or clumps do.

New methods followed. Lensfit used Bayesian model fitting with priors drawn from data to estimate shears for faint galaxies, and became the shape-measurement method for CFHTLenS and later KiDS. The general conclusion of the decade was that no method is exactly unbiased, so every survey must calibrate its residual bias against image simulations closely matched to its own data. In the following decade metacalibration (Huff & Mandelbaum 2017; Sheldon & Huff 2017) took a different approach, measuring the response of shapes to shear directly from the data by artificially shearing the observed images. DES adopted it.

Two other systematics were identified in this period and remain important. The first is photometric redshifts. Catastrophic outliers, galaxies whose colours are consistent with a very different redshift, can bias results significantly, so calibrating redshift distributions is as important as calibrating shapes. The second is baryonic physics: predictions came from dark-matter-only simulations, but gas redistributes matter on small scales. Van Daalen et al. (2011), using the OWLS simulations, showed that feedback from active galactic nuclei suppresses the matter power spectrum by tens of percent on small scales; this is a large effect for lensing and remains a major modelling uncertainty.

By 2010 blind tests, image simulations, null tests and explicit error budgets were routine in the field, and by the mid-2010s, with DES Science Verification and KiDS-450, surveys were also blinding their cosmological results.

+ More detail: B-modes as the null test

To first order, gravitational lensing produces a curl-free E-mode shear field. Any measured B-mode is therefore a systematic, and the B-mode power spectrum became the field’s standard internal null test, the lensing equivalent of a closed shutter. Chapter 6 has the picture and the reason it works.

+ More detail: why surveys started blinding themselves

By the end of this decade the systematics had become subtle enough that an analyst could unconsciously tune them toward an expected answer: stop hunting for the bug when the number looks right, keep hunting when it does not. The response was to hide the answer from the people producing it, by shifting the catalogue, the theory prediction, or the parameter axes by an unknown offset until the analysis is frozen and every null test has passed.

Blinding costs nothing in statistical power and removes a whole class of error, and by the 2010s every major survey did it routinely. This is relevant to Chapter 12: when KiDS-Legacy reported a result that moved the tension with Planck by more than two sigma, the team had committed to the analysis before seeing the number, which is why the shift could be reported plainly and was not argued over.

Chapter 11 2004 – 2015

Dark matter made visible

While the survey community worked through systematics, weak lensing produced its best-known results by a different route: mass maps of individual systems, which showed the distribution of dark matter directly.

By the early 2000s most cosmologists accepted dark matter, but the evidence was indirect. Rotation curves, cluster dynamics, X-ray gas and lensing masses all showed that visible matter cannot account for the gravity if general relativity and Newtonian gravity hold on those scales. A minority argued that gravity itself was the thing to change, and Milgrom’s Modified Newtonian Dynamics explained galaxy rotation curves well with no dark matter at all, and at the time often better than dark matter models did.

For years MOND could not be tested with lensing, because it was a non-relativistic theory and said nothing about light. That changed in 2004, when Bekenstein published TeVeS, a relativistic version. Both sides could now make lensing predictions, and the question could be put in a testable form: is there any system in which the mass seen by lensing lies clearly somewhere else than the visible matter? In a modified-gravity universe without dark matter, the gravitational field should follow the baryons. With dark matter, the two can come apart.

Fig. 11  Schematic, not to scale. The gas holds several times more ordinary matter than the stars do, so it is where most of the cluster’s baryons are. Galaxies are minute compared with the space between them and pass through essentially untouched; the two gas clouds collide, slow, and fall behind, the smaller one driving the bow shock that gives the system its name. If the gravitational field simply followed the ordinary matter, the lensing signal would peak between the two groups. It peaks on the galaxies instead.

The Bullet Cluster, 1E 0657−558, was identified in X-rays in the mid-1990s, and Chandra observations by Markevitch and collaborators in 2002 showed that it consists of two clusters that passed through each other roughly 150 million years ago, almost exactly in the plane of the sky. The smaller one’s hot gas forms a bullet-shaped cloud with a sharp bow shock ahead of it, indicating motion at about Mach 3.

A cluster has three components that behave quite differently in a collision. Galaxies are minute compared with the space between them, so they pass through essentially without colliding. Hot gas behaves as a fluid: the two clouds collide, are slowed by ram pressure, and lag behind. This gas holds most of the ordinary matter in a cluster, several times more than the stars in galaxies. Dark matter, if collisionless as standard theory assumes, should pass through like the galaxies. So after the collision the bulk of the ordinary matter sits in the middle, between two groups of galaxies. If gravity follows ordinary matter, lensing should peak on the gas. If dark matter dominates, lensing should peak on the galaxies.

Clowe, Gonzalez & Markevitch produced the first weak lensing map from ground-based imaging in 2004, finding the mass peaks offset from the gas at about 3.4σ. In 2006 Clowe and collaborators, in a paper titled “A Direct Empirical Proof of the Existence of Dark Matter,” combined deeper imaging from Magellan, the ESO Wide Field Imager and HST’s ACS, and found the two lensing peaks coincident with the galaxy concentrations and offset from the X-ray gas peaks at 8σ. A companion paper by Bradač and collaborators combined weak and strong lensing and reached the same conclusion with a more detailed map.

The argument was designed to work without assuming general relativity. In any gravity theory where the field is sourced by the visible matter, the lensing should follow the gas, because that is where most of the baryons are, and it does not. The press-release image, with X-ray gas in pink and lensing mass in blue, became one of the most reproduced pictures in astronomy.

MOND advocates replied that the result does not rule out modified gravity combined with some unseen matter, and Angus and collaborators (2007) argued that MOND could fit the Bullet Cluster if clusters contain substantial dark matter in the form of roughly 2 eV neutrinos. Many found this unappealing, since it reintroduces dark matter while keeping modified gravity, and later neutrino mass limits have made it harder to sustain. There was also a debate about the collision speed: the shock speed of roughly 4,700 km/s seemed high for ΛCDM, until simulations by Springel & Farrar (2007) showed that the shock moves faster than the dark matter halo, which relieved most of the tension.

Other merging clusters followed. MACS J0025.4−1222 (Bradač et al. 2008) showed the same separation. Abell 520, the “Train Wreck,” was more puzzling: Mahdavi et al. (2007) and Jee et al. (2012) found a mass concentration coinciding with the gas and few galaxies, which seemed to contradict the Bullet Cluster, though Clowe et al. (2012) disputed this with a different analysis. These results complicated the picture without overturning the conclusion drawn from the Bullet Cluster.

The other main result of this period came from a deep survey field. The Cosmic Evolution Survey (COSMOS) imaged a two-square-degree field with HST’s ACS in about 590 orbits, the largest contiguous HST survey at the time, with deep multi-wavelength data from X-ray to radio and photometric redshifts in dozens of bands. It was designed for studies of galaxy evolution, but the combination also suited lensing well: space-based image quality gives a stable PSF and a high density of resolved background galaxies, while the multi-band photometry gives accurate redshifts.

Massey, Rhodes, Ellis, Scoville and collaborators used about half a million galaxies for “Dark matter maps reveal cosmic scaffolding” (Nature, 2007). The new step was to use photometric redshifts to go beyond a projected map: because the lensing kernel depends on source distance, splitting the sources into redshift slices and comparing the signal across them estimates where along the line of sight the mass lies. In effect, the tomography used for power spectra was here applied to a map.

The resulting map extended to redshift about 1 and showed a network of clumps and filaments, with the dark matter generally coinciding with concentrations of galaxies and with X-ray gas seen by XMM-Newton. It was the first map in dark matter of the cosmic web predicted by simulations, which until then had been inferred from galaxies, and it traced structure becoming more clumpy over time, as gravitational growth requires.

The map has limitations. Resolution in the radial direction is poor, because the lensing kernel is broad, so the three-dimensional structure is smeared along the line of sight; the map is noisy, and individual features at modest significance deserve caution. Even so, it demonstrated the combination of space-based shapes and good photometric redshifts on which Euclid and Roman are built.

Finding clusters by mass

Around the same time, lensing began to be used to discover clusters as well as to weigh known ones. Wittman et al. (2001) found the first shear-selected cluster in the Deep Lens Survey, and larger samples followed from the Deep Lens Survey and from Subaru. Selecting by mass avoids the biases of selecting by light or X-rays, although projection effects from unrelated structures along the line of sight produce false peaks. Peak counts later became a cosmological statistic in their own right, connecting back to the non-Gaussian ideas of Chapter 8.

The ring in Cl 0024+17

Jee et al. (2007) reported a ring-shaped dark matter structure in the cluster Cl 0024+17, interpreted as a ripple from a collision along the line of sight, and it received wide publicity. Critics pointed out that such a feature could arise as an artefact of the reconstruction, particularly from the handling of the PSF and the mass-sheet degeneracy, and later analyses did not convincingly confirm it. Because mass maps are noisy reconstructions, unusual features need independent confirmation. The ring has not been recovered in later, deeper data, and the episode is now cited mainly as a warning.

Dependence on earlier methods

Both results depended on methods described in earlier chapters: the Kaiser–Squires inversion, careful PSF correction and, for COSMOS, photometric redshifts and tomography. The Bullet Cluster made weak lensing a source of direct physical evidence for dark matter, independent of assumptions about the form of gravity, and it became the result for which the field is best known outside astronomy. COSMOS showed that lensing could map the cosmic web in three dimensions, validating the approach the large surveys would take. Neither would have been possible with the methods available in 1990; both apply the techniques of Chapters 6 and 10 to a single field.

+ More detail: what cluster collisions show about dark matter self-interaction, and how the limits have changed

The Bullet Cluster also tests whether dark matter is really collisionless. If the particles scattered off one another, the dark halos would be slowed and offset from the galaxies, and the smaller cluster would lose mass. Markevitch et al. (2004) used the observed alignment to set an upper limit on the self-interaction cross-section per unit mass of roughly 1 cm²/g.

Randall et al. (2008) are often described as tightening that limit to 1.25 cm²/g, but 1.25 is a weaker offset limit than 1. Their simulations were more realistic, which made the constraint more reliable rather than tighter. They did derive a stronger limit of about 0.7 cm²/g from a different argument: the smaller cluster has not lost the mass that frequent scattering would strip away.

Harvey, Massey, Kitching, Taylor and Tittley (Science, 2015) then stacked 72 subcluster collisions in 30 systems observed with HST and Chandra, measuring the offsets between each subcluster’s gas, galaxies and lensing mass. The dark matter stays with the galaxies, and they converted this into σ/m below about 0.47 cm²/g, widely reported as the new benchmark.

That limit then loosened. It had relied on a simple model of how a drag force shifts dark matter relative to galaxies. Robertson, Massey and Eke (2017) simulated Bullet-like collisions with self-interacting dark matter and found that the galaxies, being bound to the dark matter, get dragged along with it, so the offset stays small even for sizeable cross-sections. The simple model had overstated how large an offset to expect. Kim, Peter and Wittman (2017) similarly found that the offsets are small and depend on the stage of the merger at which the system is observed. Wittman, Golovich and Dawson (2018) reanalysed the Harvey sample with more careful merger geometry and found the data support only a limit near 2 cm²/g, several times weaker than the 2015 claim.

So merging clusters today give a limit of order 1 cm²/g with real modelling uncertainty. The Bullet Cluster remains a clean qualitative demonstration of collisionless behaviour, but it is no longer the strongest quantitative constraint. Those now come from the density profiles of relaxed clusters: self-interactions carry heat inward and create constant-density cores, so the absence of large cores sets a limit. Analyses combining strong and weak lensing with stellar kinematics of the central galaxy point to values of order 0.1 cm²/g at the collision speeds typical of clusters, around 1,000 to 1,500 km/s. Examples are Kaplinghat, Tulin & Yu (2016) and Andrade et al. (2022), who give a 95% upper limit of 0.35 cm²/g.

A single number can be misleading, because many particle physics models predict a cross-section that falls with collision speed. Dwarf galaxies, with speeds of tens of km/s, may favour a few cm²/g to explain their cored profiles, while clusters demand far less. Modern limits are therefore quoted at a particular velocity, and the combination of dwarf, galaxy, group and cluster constraints maps out the velocity dependence rather than yielding one figure.

Chapter 12 2012 – present

The survey era

Current surveys measure the shapes of a hundred million galaxies to a fraction of a percent, in analyses kept blind until they are complete. Weak lensing is now a precision cosmological probe, and for a decade its results were in mild tension with the microwave background.

The Dark Energy Task Force sorted experiments into stages. Stage III covered the ground-based surveys of the 2010s, each covering hundreds to thousands of square degrees, and each designed around the lessons of Chapter 10: shapes calibrated against image simulations, redshift distributions calibrated carefully, intrinsic alignments and baryonic effects modelled explicitly, and results blinded until the analysis was frozen.

CFHTLenS. The CFHT Legacy Survey Wide field covered about 154 square degrees, and its first analyses had run into the residual systematics that work in the previous decade had warned about. The CFHTLenS team, led by Catherine Heymans and Ludovic van Waerbeke, reprocessed everything from the raw pixels, with new PSF modelling, lensfit shapes and new photometric redshifts, tested each pointing for systematics and discarded about a quarter of them. Kilbinger et al. (2013) and Heymans et al. (2013), the latter with six-bin tomography and intrinsic alignments included, produced the first cosmic shear constraints the community broadly trusted. This approach, reprocessing from pixels, running explicit null tests and blinding the result, became the model for later surveys.

The results disagreed with Planck. Planck’s 2013 and 2015 results fixed the parameters of ΛCDM with great precision, and extrapolated forward they predicted S₈ ≈ 0.83; CFHTLenS came out lower. Several other low-redshift probes, including Planck’s own Sunyaev–Zel’dovich cluster counts, also sat below the prediction. MacCrann et al. (2015) quantified it and found a modest discrepancy that could not easily be fixed by extending the model.

Newer surveys found the same pattern. KiDS-450 (Hildebrandt et al. 2017), from the Kilo-Degree Survey on ESO’s VLT Survey Telescope, found S₈ = 0.745 ± 0.039, a 2.3σ difference from Planck 2015 in S₈. KiDS was purpose-built for lensing. KiDS-450 itself used four-band ugri photometry, with redshift distributions calibrated directly against deep spectroscopic surveys; the nine-band optical and near-infrared photometry that gave KiDS its unusually good photometric redshifts arrived with the VIKING data in the KV450 reanalysis (Hildebrandt et al. 2020), which found much the same answer and so made the disagreement harder to dismiss.

DES

DES used the Dark Energy Camera, on the same Blanco telescope Wittman and Tyson had used in 2000. It made the 3×2pt analysis from Chapter 7 (cosmic shear, galaxy clustering and galaxy–galaxy lensing combined) the standard, alongside the contemporaneous KiDS-450 combinations with GAMA and 2dFLenS (van Uitert et al. 2018; Joudaki et al. 2018), and it introduced metacalibration shapes. Y1 cosmic shear gave S₈ = 0.782 ± 0.027 (Troxel et al. 2018) and the Y1 3×2pt analysis 0.773 (+0.026/−0.020), judged compatible with Planck (Abbott et al. 2018). For Y3 the blind cosmic shear analysis gave S₈ = 0.759 (+0.025/−0.023) (Amon et al. 2022; Secco et al. 2022), 2.3σ from Planck 2018 (2.1σ for the ΛCDM-optimised analysis), which the team described as statistically consistent, and the Y3 3×2pt result was S₈ = 0.776 ± 0.017 with Ωm = 0.339 (Abbott et al. 2022).

KiDS

KiDS-1000 (Asgari et al. 2021; Heymans et al. 2021), with redshift calibration improved using self-organising maps, found S₈ = 0.759 (+0.024/−0.021), about 3σ from Planck. That was the peak of the disagreement, and the point at which it was taken most seriously as possible new physics. The 3×2pt analysis by Heymans et al. (2021), adding BOSS and 2dFLenS galaxy clustering, gave S₈ = 0.766 (+0.020/−0.014): it matched Planck’s precision but sat 8.3 ± 2.6% below it, leaving the disagreement in place.

HSC, and a joint look

Hyper Suprime-Cam on the 8-metre Subaru covers less area but goes much deeper; its Y3 analyses gave S₈ = 0.776 (+0.032/−0.033) from the shear power spectra (Dalal et al. 2023), roughly 2σ from Planck 2018, and 0.769 (+0.031/−0.034) from the correlation functions (Li et al. 2023). Then a joint DES Y3 and KiDS-1000 reanalysis in 2023 harmonised the modelling choices between the two teams and found S₈ = 0.790 (+0.018/−0.014), in agreement with Planck at 1.7σ. This was the first clear sign that a real part of the discrepancy came from analysis choices.

Four explanations were proposed, each connected to earlier chapters. A statistical fluctuation: each result was only two to three sigma from Planck, but they all pointed the same way. Systematics: the candidates were intrinsic alignments, photometric redshift errors (the faint blue galaxies of Chapter 5 again) and residual shear bias. Baryonic feedback: Amon & Efstathiou (2022) argued that stronger small-scale suppression of the matter power spectrum than standard models assume could reconcile lensing with Planck without any new physics, a proposal that builds on van Daalen’s 2011 result. Finally, new physics: interacting dark matter, dark energy affecting the growth of structure, modified gravity.

The tension has since eased considerably. KiDS-Legacy (Wright et al. 2025) analysed the final survey, 1,347 square degrees of nine-band imaging, with better redshift calibration and multi-band image simulations that let the team extend the source sample to photometric redshift 2. It found S₈ = 0.815 (+0.016/−0.021), agreeing with Planck to within 0.73σ. The authors noted how large a shift this was. Compared with the most similar earlier KiDS analysis, the tension dropped by 2.2σ; the team had committed to the analysis choices behind it before unblinding and still found the change surprising. About two-thirds of the movement towards Planck came from improved estimation and calibration of the redshift distributions. A 2025 reanalysis of HSC Y3 cosmic shear using clustering redshifts from DESI pointed the same way: S₈ rose to 0.805 ± 0.018, with uncertainties 1.8 times smaller than before, and together with KiDS-Legacy this points to redshift calibration as the key systematic. A companion paper found that KiDS-Legacy combined with DES Y3 cosmic shear, supernovae and DESI baryon acoustic oscillations gives S₈ = 0.814, consistent with Planck.

DES Y6, released in January 2026, is the final DES result. Its 3×2pt analysis covers about 5,000 square degrees, using 140 million source galaxy shapes and 9 million lens galaxies, and finds S₈ = 0.789 ± 0.012 with Ωm = 0.333 (+0.023/−0.028); cosmic shear alone gives S₈ = 0.783 (+0.019/−0.015). That is 1.8σ from a combination of Planck 2018, ACT DR6 and SPT-3G in the full parameter space, and 2.6σ when projected onto S₈ alone. The shape measurements show the progress of the programme described in Chapter 10: with the new metadetection method, the team found no detectable multiplicative bias at roughly the half-percent level, which is not far from the 0.1% goal set around 2006.

At present weak lensing still tends to come out slightly below the microwave background, but the gap now runs from no tension in KiDS-Legacy to about 2.6σ in S₈ for DES Y6, and part of the spread between surveys comes from conventions: HSC, KiDS and DES summarise their posteriors differently, which alone can shift a quoted value by about 0.01, and the quoted tension depends on which CMB baseline is used. Over the decade the lensing values rose from about 0.74–0.76 to about 0.79–0.82 while the uncertainties shrank from about ±0.04 to ±0.012. The field is moving away from the language of tension and toward a narrower question: whether the small remaining offset reflects baryonic physics.

Stage IV has just begun. Euclid, the ESA mission, is in effect COSMOS scaled up to a third of the sky. It launched on 1 July 2023, with quick releases in March 2025 and June 2026; in November 2026 it releases raw and calibrated data over about 1,900 square degrees, and the full first data release follows in mid-2027, with its first cosmic shear cosmology after that.

Rubin Observatory’s LSST, the ground-based counterpart, will image the southern sky repeatedly over ten years and is now in early operations.

Together these should measure billions of galaxy shapes, which will push statistical errors well below the systematics identified in Chapters 5, 6 and 10. How much they achieve will depend on photometric redshifts, the blending of overlapping galaxies, intrinsic alignments and baryons.

Kristian and Sachs predicted in 1966 that a lumpy universe would coherently distort the images of distant galaxies. Sixty years later, surveys measure that distortion in more than a hundred million of them, with sub-percent shear calibration, and test ΛCDM at a level comparable to Planck.

Each stage built on the one before it. The faint blue galaxies supplied the sources and Kaiser–Squires the method, and the four detections of 2000 showed that the signal was real. Precision came from a decade of work on systematics: blind challenges, image simulations, null tests and error budgets.

+ More detail: baryonic feedback as an explanation for the S₈ offset

Lensing is most sensitive on scales of a few arcminutes, which correspond to structures of a few hundred kiloparsecs to a few megaparsecs. These are the scales on which galaxy formation rearranges matter: gas cools into galaxies, then active galactic nuclei and supernovae drive it back out, redistributing mass over the scales lensing measures best.

Dark-matter-only simulations cannot capture this, and van Daalen et al. (2011) showed that the effect is large, suppressing the matter power spectrum by tens of percent on small scales. Amon & Efstathiou (2022) reversed the usual approach. Instead of treating the suppression as a nuisance to be marginalised over, they supposed that it is stronger than standard models assume. In that case lensing and Planck agree without new physics, and the S₈ offset becomes a measurement of feedback instead of evidence against ΛCDM.

The explanation is attractive because it accounts for the offset appearing in lensing and not in the microwave background, which probes far larger scales at a far earlier time. The difficulty is that the required feedback strength has to be checked independently, against X-ray and Sunyaev–Zel’dovich observations of the gas around galaxies and groups and against hydrodynamic simulations that do not agree with each other. It remains under discussion, and it is the reason the field now frames the question as one about astrophysics rather than cosmology.
